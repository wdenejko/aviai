Teaching a small AI to read the weather

01 — The task

Where a general-purpose LLM breaks on a METAR

Decoding a METAR to structured JSON is trivial for a dispatcher and for a 200-line parser. An off-the-shelf LLM handles most of it too — station, wind, temperature, cloud state — then falls over on exactly two things: the unit conversions, and knowing when to stay silent rather than invent a value. Why this task — the honest reason

Because there's a perfect answer key: the three-parser consensus + the official NOAA decode. That lets us grade the model automatically on thousands of cases, so every claim is measured, not asserted — an ideal sandbox for learning finetuning. (Whether you'd ship the model instead of the parser is a separate question — §6.)

02 — The core idea

What "finetuning" actually means

An AI language model comes in two life stages, and the difference is the whole point of this page.

StageHow it's madeWhat it's like
Base modelTrained once, by Google, on a huge slice of the internet A brilliant generalist — knows a bit about everything, expert at nothing specific
Finetuned modelYou take the base model and train it a little more, on examples of your task An apprentice who has now practised your exact job thousands of times

Finetuning is not building an AI from scratch — that costs millions and needs a data centre. It's taking a finished, off-the-shelf model and giving it focused practice. We handed the base model 3,000 worked examples — raw METAR in, correct JSON out — and let it practise for one pass, with every example emphasising the thing it was bad at: the conversions.

The trick that makes it cheap: LoRA

A base model has billions of numbers ("weights") — its brain. Retraining all of them is slow and expensive. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) freezes the entire original brain and bolts on a tiny set of new numbers — an adapter — that nudge its behaviour. We trained just 0.49% of the model.

The 5-billion-weight base model stays frozen; only the small adapter is trained — so the whole thing fits on a single desktop GPU and finishes in under an hour, producing a 50 MB file you attach at run time.

The shortcut: instead of rewiring the AI's entire brain, LoRA clips on a small "cheat sheet" the model consults — cheap to make, tiny to store, and the original model stays untouched for other tasks.

03 — Measuring it honestly

How do you know it worked?

Anyone can claim their AI "got better." The hard, important part of this project is proving it — with numbers you can trust. Three ideas make that possible.

1 · A locked test set the model never sees

We set aside 620 weather reports — from airports and dates the model never trained on — and froze them (a cryptographic fingerprint guarantees they never change). Every model is graded on this exact same set. Testing on unseen data is how you tell real learning from mere memorisation.

2 · Three numbers, because "accuracy" hides the danger

In a safety domain, how a model is wrong matters as much as how often — so we track three things separately:

MetricThe question it answersWhy it matters
Value accuracyOf the facts that were really there, how many did it get right?The headline "how good is it"
HallucinationHow often did it invent a value that wasn't in the report?The dangerous kind of wrong — a made-up wind gust could mislead a pilot
Whole-record accuracyHow often was the entire report perfect, every field at once?The strictest bar — one slip fails the whole report

A model that says "I don't know" is making a safe mistake; a model that confidently makes up a number is making a dangerous one. We measure those two separately on purpose — folding them into one "accuracy" score would hide exactly the thing that matters most in aviation.

3 · The answer key, and proof the win is real

How do we know the "right" answer for 620 reports × 11 fields without a human labelling all of them? A panel of three independent parsers plus the official government decode — we only trust a field when they agree (a "trust ladder"). And to be sure an improvement isn't luck, a statistical test (McNemar's) looks only at where two models disagree, counting fixes vs. new mistakes and returning a confidence number.

What "p ≈ 10⁻³¹²" means

Our results came back with p-values like 0.000…(312 zeros)…1. In plain terms: the chance this improvement is a fluke is essentially zero. When a finetune works, it really works — for the 2B model, 1,437 fields fixed against just 3 made worse.

05 — The parts nobody warns you about

Two gotchas that ate an afternoon

Finetuning the model is maybe half the work. Deploying it — getting the trained model to actually run and answer cleanly — is the other half, and where the sneaky bugs live.

Gotcha 1 · The model wanted to "think out loud"

Gemma 4 is a reasoning model — left alone it writes out its thought process before answering. For a quick structured decode that's a waste; the bigger model would ramble for hundreds of words and run out of room before giving the JSON. We had to explicitly tell it to answer directly.

Gotcha 2 · A one-character mismatch broke everything

The finetuned model was quietly failing a quarter of the time — spitting out gibberish instead of JSON. The cause was subtle: it had memorised the exact format of its training prompts, but the serving software rebuilt that prompt with a slightly different tool, producing a marginally different string. The over-drilled model got confused. Feeding it the byte-for-byte training format fixed it instantly — from 73% usable to 100%.

A heavily-drilled apprentice can become brittle — thrown off by a tiny change in how the question is phrased, even when it means the same thing. Whatever exact wording you train with, you must reproduce exactly when you deploy. "Close enough" isn't.

06 — The honest verdict

So… is it actually useful?

Here's the twist, and it's the most important thing to take away from this whole demo.

The finetune is a runaway success as a demonstration — huge, statistically bulletproof gains. And yet, for this task, you would not ship it. A 200-line hand-written parser already decodes METARs at ~100%, instantly, for free, and never makes anything up. The AI, even finetuned, only catches up to the parser it was trained to imitate.

That's not a failure — it's the correct, honest reading. Our test grades the AI against the parser's answers, so by design the parser is the ceiling. This measures how well finetuning can make a model match a known-good tool, not beat it.

So when does finetuning a small model earn its keep?

  • When there is no parser — messy, non-standard, or free-text input that rigid programs choke on. That's where an AI's flexibility actually wins (and the natural next experiment).
  • When you need one small, private, cheap model you own and run yourself — no per-call API fees, no data leaving your machine.
  • As the fastest way to learn the craft — this task has a perfect answer key, so every step is measurable. It's a flight simulator for finetuning: the ideal place to make your mistakes.
The one-sentence takeaway: finetuning reliably teaches a small model a specific skill, cheaply and measurably — but whether it's worth it depends entirely on whether a simpler tool already does the job. Know which world you're in before you train.
07 — Glossary

Every term, in one place

Base model
A ready-made AI (here, Google's Gemma 4) trained by someone else on general data. Your starting point.
Finetuning
Training a base model a little more on examples of your specific task, to specialise it.
LoRA / adapter
A cheap finetuning method: freeze the big model, train a tiny add-on (~0.5% of the size) that steers it.
Epoch
One full pass through all your training examples. We did just one — that was enough.
Eval set
A locked collection of held-out examples used to grade every model fairly and identically.
Hallucination
When a model invents a fact that wasn't in the input. The dangerous kind of error.
Whole-record accuracy
The strict score: the entire output is correct, every field at once.
McNemar's test
A statistics check that says whether "after" truly beats "before," or if it's chance.
Quantization / GGUF
Shrinking a model (rounding its numbers) so it runs fast on modest hardware. GGUF is the file format we served.
METAR
The coded hourly weather report every airport publishes. The thing we're decoding.
